Protein generation with evolutionary diffusion (Evodiff)

Breaking the structure-first paradigm with evolutionary diffusion

Protein design is entering a new era with EvoDiff, a sequence-based diffusion framework that challenges the dominant structure-first approach. While state-of-the-art methods like RFdiffusion generate protein backbones then design sequences to fold into them, EvoDiff operates directly in sequence space—training on 42 million evolutionary sequences rather than ~200,000 experimental structures. This enables generation of proteins inaccessible to structure-based models, including intrinsically disordered regions, while better capturing natural sequence and functional diversity.

The core innovation is applying discrete diffusion to protein sequences at evolutionary scale: corrupting sequences through biologically-informed transition matrices or order-agnostic masking, then learning to reverse this corruption. The result is a framework achieving comparable designability to structure-based methods while accessing the full breadth of natural protein space—a space two orders of magnitude larger than what structural databases represent.

Why sequence-first design matters for protein generation

The standard paradigm in computational protein design follows a two-step pipeline: generate a target 3D structure, then use inverse folding to design sequences that will adopt that fold. This structure-function framework has produced remarkable successes—RFdiffusion has generated experimentally validated binders with picomolar affinity, and ProteinMPNN achieves 52.4% sequence recovery on native backbones, a substantial improvement over Rosetta’s 32.9%.

However, this paradigm has fundamental limitations. The Protein Data Bank contains roughly 200,000 experimental structures, representing a sparse and biased subset of the estimated billions of unique natural proteins. Proteins that crystallize well are overrepresented; those that don’t—including the 30-50% of eukaryotic proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)—are largely absent. Structure-based generative models inherit these biases: both ESM-2 and RFdiffusion generate proteins enriched in α-helices while underrepresenting β-strands and disordered regions.

More fundamentally, not every protein folds into a static structure. IDRs carry out essential biological functions through their disorder—mediating protein-protein interactions, signal transduction, and transcriptional regulation without adopting fixed conformations. For these proteins, the structure-function paradigm breaks down entirely: there is no stable backbone to generate, and inverse folding methods cannot operate on coordinates that don’t exist.

How discrete diffusion works for protein sequences

EvoDiff adapts diffusion models from continuous to discrete domains, operating on amino acid tokens rather than 3D coordinates. The framework explores two discrete diffusion approaches, with important differences in their mathematical formulations and performance characteristics.

D3PM: Discrete denoising diffusion with transition matrices

Discrete Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (D3PM) define a forward corruption process through transition matrices. For a protein sequence, each amino acid $x_t$ at timestep $t$ transitions according to:

\[q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \text{Cat}(x_t; p = x_{t-1} Q_t)\]

where $Q_t$ is a $\mathrm{K \times K}$ transition matrix ($K=21$ for amino acids plus gap token) controlling how tokens mutate at each step. After $T$ steps, the sequence becomes indistinguishable from the stationary distribution.

EvoDiff implements two transition matrix variants:

  • D3PM-Uniform: Equal probability of transitioning to any amino acid, converging to a uniform stationary distribution
  • D3PM-BLOSUM: Transition probabilities derived from BLOSUM62 substitution matrices, incorporating evolutionary relationships between amino acids

The BLOSUM-based approach is biologically motivated—amino acids with similar physicochemical properties (e.g., leucine and isoleucine) have higher transition probabilities, mirroring natural evolutionary substitution patterns. The transition matrix is made doubly stochastic via Sinkhorn-Knopp normalization, with a β-schedule tapering mutations to corrupt half the sequence by $t=250$ of $T=500$ timesteps.

OADM: Order-agnostic autoregressive diffusion

Order-Agnostic Autoregressive Diffusion Models (OADM) take a different approach: rather than mutating tokens, they progressively mask positions in random order. This connects discrete diffusion to the masked language modeling paradigm familiar from BERT and ESM-2, but with a crucial generalization.

Standard autoregressive models factorize the joint distribution left-to-right:

\[\log p(x) = \sum_{t=1}^L \log p(x_t | x_{\lt t})\]

OADM removes the left-to-right constraint by training over all possible decoding orders:

\[\log p(x) ≥ E_{σ ~ \text{Uniform}(S_L)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^L \log p(x_{σ(t)} | x_{σ(\lt t)}) \right]\]

where $σ$ is a random permutation sampled uniformly from all $L!$ orderings. The model learns to reconstruct masked positions given arbitrary context, enabling generation in any order and—critically—natural conditioning by simply fixing some positions during inference.

Why OADM outperforms D3PM

A key empirical finding is that EvoDiff-OADM consistently outperforms EvoDiff-D3PM on test-set perplexity and downstream generation quality. OADM is also the only variant where performance scales with model size—from 38M to 640M parameters.

The intuition is that OADM presents an easier learning task. In D3PM, the model must learn to reverse arbitrary mutations at each timestep, handling the combinatorics of multiple corrupted positions. In OADM, the task is always the same: predict masked tokens given unmasked context—precisely the masked language modeling objective that has proven highly effective for protein representation learning.

Model architecture and training at evolutionary scale

EvoDiff uses a ByteNet-style dilated convolutional architecture rather than the transformers common in protein language models. This choice reflects prior work showing competitive performance with transformers for protein sequence pretraining while offering better computational efficiency for long sequences.

Sequence models (EvoDiff-Seq)

The sequence models are trained on 42 million sequences from UniRef50, using:

  • Architecture: Dilated CNN with 38M or 640M parameters
  • Model dimension: 1280 (for 640M variant)
  • Layers: 56 dilated convolutional layers
  • Training: Adam optimizer, 1e-4 learning rate, linear warmup over 16K steps
  • Compute: 32 NVIDIA V100 GPUs for 23 days (still not converged)

The 640M-parameter OADM model (designated EvoDiff-Seq) achieves test-set perplexity of 13.05, compared to 15.74 for D3PM-BLOSUM and 18.47 for D3PM-Uniform.

MSA models (EvoDiff-MSA)

To leverage deeper evolutionary information, EvoDiff also includes MSA-conditioned models trained on OpenFold’s dataset of 382,296 multiple sequence alignments:

  • Architecture: MSA Transformer with ~100M parameters
  • Inputs: 64 sequences × 512 residues (subsampled via MaxHamming for diversity)
  • Capability: Generate novel query sequences conditioned on evolutionary context from an MSA

The MSA conditioning enables evolution-guided generation: given related sequences from a protein family, EvoDiff-MSA can generate novel members that respect family-specific constraints while exploring sequence space beyond observed natural variation.

Evaluation framework: Beyond designability metrics

Evaluating generative protein models requires assessing multiple dimensions: individual sequence validity, distributional coverage of natural protein space, and functional capability for design tasks.

Structural plausibility assessment

For individual sequences, EvoDiff uses structure prediction (OmegaFold) followed by inverse folding (ESM-IF) to compute self-consistency:

  1. Foldability (pLDDT): Predict structure for generated sequence; high pLDDT indicates confident, likely foldable structure
  2. Self-consistency perplexity (scPerplexity): Inverse fold the predicted structure; low perplexity indicates the sequence is likely to fold to that structure

EvoDiff-Seq achieves median pLDDT ~44 and scPerplexity ~11.5, with many individual generations showing high quality (pLDDT >80, scPerplexity <5). While no generative model matches the natural test set, EvoDiff-OADM outperforms other diffusion variants and masked language model baselines.

Distributional coverage of natural protein space

A key advantage of sequence-based generation is accessing broader protein diversity. Using ProtT5 embeddings (which capture functional annotations), EvoDiff measures Fréchet ProtT5 Distance (FPD) between generated and natural sequences:

Model FPD (lower = better)
EvoDiff-Seq 0.88
RFdiffusion + ESM-IF 1.96
ESM-2 (Gibbs sampling) 2.81

The structural distributions are equally revealing. Analyzing DSSP secondary structure predictions, EvoDiff-Seq generates proportions of β-strands and disordered regions matching natural proteins—unlike ESM-2 and RFdiffusion, which are both enriched in α-helices. This reflects EvoDiff’s training on the full diversity of UniRef50 rather than structure-biased PDB data.

Motif scaffolding without explicit structural information

A central application of structure-based diffusion is motif scaffolding: given 3D coordinates of a functional motif (enzyme active site, binding epitope), generate a scaffold that holds the motif in precise geometry. This is where EvoDiff makes a provocative claim: structural information may not be necessary.

Sequence-only scaffolding methodology

EvoDiff approaches scaffolding purely through conditional generation in sequence space:

  1. Fix the motif’s amino acid sequence (not coordinates)
  2. Mask scaffold positions
  3. Generate scaffold sequence via EvoDiff’s reverse diffusion process
  4. Predict structure of complete sequence (OmegaFold)
  5. Evaluate motif RMSD against original crystal structure

Success criteria (from RFdiffusion benchmarks): pLDDT ≥ 70 and motif RMSD ≤ 1.0Å.

Performance comparison with RFdiffusion

Across 17 scaffolding problems spanning viral epitopes, receptor traps, enzyme active sites, and binding sites:

Model Successful scaffolds (of 1700 total attempts)
RFdiffusion 610
EvoDiff-MSA 522
EvoDiff-Seq 149

The correlation between problem-specific success rates is notably weak—EvoDiff and RFdiffusion have orthogonal strengths. Problems where RFdiffusion excels (e.g., 2KL8, 6EXZ) show zero EvoDiff successes, while EvoDiff succeeds on problems RFdiffusion fails (e.g., 5YUI, 1QJG). This suggests sequence-based and structure-based scaffolding access fundamentally different regions of design space.

The MSA-conditioned model substantially outperforms sequence-only generation, with EvoDiff-MSA achieving success on 13 of 17 problems compared to 8 for EvoDiff-Seq. Evolutionary context from related sequences provides implicit structural constraints without explicit coordinates.

Generating intrinsically disordered regions: The unique capability

Perhaps EvoDiff’s most distinctive capability is generating intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)—something structure-based methods fundamentally cannot do.

Experimental design

Using a dataset of 15,996 human IDRs mapped to full protein sequences:

  1. Provide full protein sequence with IDR region masked
  2. Generate replacement IDR sequence via EvoDiff inpainting
  3. Evaluate disorder prediction on generated sequence (DR-BERT)
  4. Compare to scrambled and randomly-sampled baselines

Results: Preserved disorder character

Generated IDRs show disorder scores closely matching true IDRs and substantially exceeding random/scrambled baselines. EvoDiff-MSA achieves Pearson correlation R=0.67 between original and generated IDR disorder scores, compared to R=0.25 for EvoDiff-Seq.

Importantly, generated IDRs are novel sequences: average similarity to original IDRs is <25% for EvoDiff-Seq and <40% for EvoDiff-MSA. The models generate functionally disordered regions without copying the original sequences.

This capability is unique among current protein generative models. Structure-based methods require backbone coordinates as input; IDRs by definition lack stable backbone geometry. EvoDiff’s sequence-first approach sidesteps this requirement entirely.

Positioning within the protein generation landscape

EvoDiff occupies a distinct niche in the rapidly evolving landscape of protein generative models. Understanding its positioning requires comparing across several dimensions.

Comparison with structure-based diffusion models

RFdiffusion remains the gold standard for structure generation, fine-tuning a pretrained RoseTTAFold network for diffusion over SE(3) residue frames. Its advantages include:

  • Highest designability rates for structured proteins
  • Extensive experimental validation (picomolar binders, crystal structures)
  • Rich conditioning mechanisms (topology, symmetry, hotspot targeting)

Its limitations—which EvoDiff addresses—include:

  • Training bias toward well-ordered, crystallizable proteins
  • Inability to generate disordered regions
  • Requires downstream inverse folding for sequences

Chroma (Illuminating protein space) offers programmable generation with natural language prompts and joint sequence-structure modeling, but shares RFdiffusion’s structural data limitations.

FrameDiff and FoldingDiff provide cleaner theoretical frameworks (SE(3) diffusion and backbone angle diffusion, respectively) but with lower designability than RFdiffusion and no native conditioning mechanisms.

Comparison with protein language models

ESM-2 (up to 15B parameters) and ProtGPT2 (738M parameters) represent the language model approach. Key differences from EvoDiff:

Aspect ESM-2 ProtGPT2 EvoDiff
Training objective Masked LM Autoregressive Discrete diffusion
Native generation No (Gibbs sampling) Yes Yes
Generation order Iterative random Left-to-right Order-agnostic
Conditioning Difficult Control tags Natural (inpainting)
Sequence length Fixed Variable Variable

ESM-2 generates via Gibbs sampling—iteratively masking and resampling positions—which is slow and lacks proper generative modeling foundations. EvoDiff’s OADM approach generalizes MLM to a proper diffusion framework with natural conditional generation.

ProGen2 (up to 6.4B parameters) uses autoregressive generation with control tags for protein family and function. While offering fast, controllable sampling, the left-to-right bias may limit diversity, and conditioning requires pretraining or fine-tuning rather than EvoDiff’s natural inpainting.

Comparison with inverse folding methods

ProteinMPNN (52.4% sequence recovery) and LM-Design (55.65%) represent state-of-the-art inverse folding. These methods are complementary to EvoDiff:

  • Inverse folding: Structure → Sequence
  • EvoDiff: Direct sequence generation (no structure required)

When structure-based generation is appropriate, the RFdiffusion + ProteinMPNN pipeline remains powerful. EvoDiff offers an alternative when:

  • Disordered regions are needed
  • Structure is not well-defined
  • Evolutionary constraints should drive design
  • Broader sequence diversity is desired

Comparison with flow matching approaches

FrameFlow and FoldFlow apply flow matching to protein backbone generation, achieving 5× faster sampling than diffusion with comparable or better designability. Flow matching offers:

  • Deterministic ODE (vs. stochastic SDE in diffusion)
  • Straighter sampling trajectories
  • Scale-invariance (no length-dependent hyperparameters)

These methods operate on structures rather than sequences, sharing RFdiffusion’s limitations for disordered proteins. A future direction could combine flow matching with discrete sequence generation.

Technical tradeoffs and when to use EvoDiff

Strengths

  1. Evolutionary-scale training data: 42M sequences vs. ~200K structures—200× more data
  2. IDR generation: Unique capability among current methods
  3. Natural conditioning: Inpainting via masking requires no architectural changes
  4. Distributional fidelity: Better matches natural sequence/structure distributions
  5. MSA conditioning: Leverages evolutionary relationships for family-aware generation

Limitations

  1. Individual sample quality: Structure-based methods achieve higher individual designability rates
  2. Experimental validation: Less extensive than RFdiffusion’s crystallography/EM validation
  3. No explicit structural control: Cannot specify 3D geometric constraints
  4. Computational requirements: 23+ days training on 32 V100 GPUs (still not converged)

Recommended use cases

Use EvoDiff when:

  • Designing proteins with intrinsically disordered regions
  • Exploring broad sequence diversity within evolutionary constraints
  • MSA-guided generation of family members
  • Rapid prototyping without structure generation pipeline

Use structure-based methods when:

  • Precise geometric constraints are critical (e.g., active site scaffolding)
  • Designing symmetric assemblies
  • Protein-protein interface design with known target structure
  • Experimental validation of designed structures is planned

Conclusion: Toward universal protein design

EvoDiff represents a conceptual shift in protein generation: from structure-first to sequence-first design. By training diffusion models on evolutionary-scale sequence data rather than structural databases, it accesses the full diversity of natural protein space—including the substantial fraction of proteins that lack stable 3D structure.

The key insight is that sequence, not structure, is the universal design space for proteins. Every protein is synthesized as a sequence; structure emerges from sequence. By operating directly in sequence space, EvoDiff can generate proteins that structure-based methods cannot access while providing natural mechanisms for evolutionary conditioning.

The comparison with structure-based scaffolding reveals complementary strengths: EvoDiff and RFdiffusion succeed on largely orthogonal problem sets. This suggests the future of protein design may involve ensembling sequence-based and structure-based approaches, leveraging each method’s unique capabilities.

Most significantly, EvoDiff’s ability to generate intrinsically disordered regions opens design possibilities for the ~30% of the proteome that current structure-based methods ignore. As our understanding of disorder-mediated function grows, this capability may prove increasingly valuable.
